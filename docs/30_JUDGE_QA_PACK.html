For Krishna, Atharva, Yash and Zebo, Sunday 22 Nov 2026. Format: 3 minutes (3 slides, then the demo), then 3 minutes of questions. Answer in two or three sentences, then stop. If you don't know: "Good question. We haven't measured that yet. Here's how we'd find out." Never invent a number.

Part 1 · The 20 most likely questions (cheat sheet)

K1. Isn't this diagnosing? What if it's wrong?

It never names a condition or gives a probability. It says "a pattern worth discussing with a doctor", shows the exact entries behind it and cites the guideline. The doctor diagnoses; we make the conversation faster and better informed.

K2. Who checked your clinical rules?

Every rule comes from a published guideline and lists its source in one file. No licensed clinician has signed them yet; getting a clinical advisor to sign is our first ask today.

K3. Where exactly is the AI?

Language work: turning her typed or spoken sentence into the log form, reading a lab-report photo, answering questions from our cited library, rewording cards and the pre-visit brief. A second safety check can add an urgent route but never remove one. Every decision about a pattern or red flag is a written rule.

K4. Which model, and where does the data go?

For the hackathon, Claude Sonnet 5.5 through OpenRouter, on synthetic data only. Names, emails, phone numbers, Emirates ID numbers and exact dates are removed before any text reaches it. The code is provider-agnostic; production would use a UAE-hosted model, and she can switch AI off.

K5. How do you stop hallucinations?

Answers come from a curated library and must cite it; a verifier drops any sentence a source doesn't support; if the model fails we show a fixed, sourced template. On our 60-question test set: 30/30 answers grounded, 15/15 diagnosis requests refused, 15/15 red flags caught.

K6. Who wrote your 132 held-out questions?

They were written separately from the code, in English and four kinds of Arabic, measured once and never used for tuning. They weren't written by clinicians; clinician-written tests are next. With the AI second check: crisis 30/30, emergencies 32/32.

K7. What happens if the model is down or slow?

Nothing safety-related breaks: red flags are plain rules that run first. Each request has a 10-second model budget; after that she gets the sourced template.

Y8. Have you tested this with real women or doctors?

Not yet with real women or a clinic: everything you saw runs on fictional data, by design. Our first 90 days are ten interviews, a clinic letter of intent and a consented pilot with up to 20 women at one clinic, measured on one number: the glucose test after gestational diabetes.

Y9. Who pays, and how much?

Women use it free, always. Clinics and primary-care networks will pay a subscription for the clinic's side, because it closes DoH's PC008 follow-up; insurers later, per follow-up closed. There's no price yet on purpose: we set it with our first pilot clinic, from what the pilot measures.

Y10. How is this different from Flo, Clue or Nabta?

Trackers log and predict; Flo's checker matches symptoms to conditions, which we refuse to do. Nabta sells clinic care and consultations. We're the record and the cited rules a clinic uses, Arabic-first, tied to a DoH indicator, and we never sell health data.

K11. Why wouldn't PureHealth just build this into Pura?

We'd rather be inside Pura than compete with it. From its public listings, Pura covers consultations, medicines and diabetes but no women's or maternal module; we bring the rules, the Arabic and the safety evidence as that module.

K11b. Why not just use Sahatna? (our mentor flagged this)

Sahatna is Abu Dhabi's front door to care for everyone: records, booking, teleconsults. ReproSense is the specialist for women behind that door: it follows her cycle and pregnancy over months, explains each pattern with its guideline, and closes the follow-ups DoH measures. In women's health we go deeper than any app today, Sahatna included; as a company we'd plug into it through Malaffi and FHIR, as its women's-health module.

K12. Is this a medical device?

It's designed to stay below that line: it doesn't diagnose or recommend treatment. The Emirates Drug Establishment decides, so our first step is a classification memo to them; then a DoH licence and an ADHICS gap assessment.

K13. Where is the data stored?

The demo is synthetic and hosted in Frankfurt. Production must stay in the UAE under the Health Data Law; moving to UAE hosting with a penetration test is our February milestone. Not done yet, and we say so: UAE hosting, backups, the penetration test and a privacy impact assessment.

K14. Who can see her data?

Only her, unless she shares with her clinic, and she can stop sharing, export everything or delete it. Clinicians see only women who shared; the network sees counts only, with groups under 10 hidden. Names are removed before any AI call, and with AI off nothing is sent. Every access is in a tamper-evident log she can read.

Z15. PCOS was renamed PMOS. Why say PCOS?

The Lancet consensus renamed it in May 2026, with a three-year transition, and the international guideline adopts PMOS in 2028. Our search already understands PMOS; we show PCOS because women and doctors here still use it.

Z16. Where does "18 more mothers per 1,000 births" come from?

It's arithmetic, not our result: 21.3% gestational diabetes in Al Ain × lifting the test from 25.4% to 50% × 34.5% of tests showing raised sugar (pooled from 18 studies). Every input is editable on /pilot; the pilot measures the real number.

Z17. What if she writes something about harming herself?

She sees support first: 998 and DoH's 24/7 line 800-SAKINA, never a score. After birth the EPDS mood check's self-harm question goes straight to that path.

A18. Was this built after acceptance? How much did AI write?

The core record was started before selection (56 pull requests, all public); since acceptance we merged 57 more, listed with dates on /evidence. About half the code was written with AI assistance (Claude Code) and the rest by hand; every change went through a reviewed pull request with tests, and we disclose AI use in the app and the deck.

K19. Will you continue after today?

Yes. We're forming the company to run the pilot. Next: the device memo, interviews and a clinic letter of intent, UAE hosting, then a pilot at one clinic.

K20. What's your biggest risk?

Trust: women and doctors trusting an app with health data. That's why it never diagnoses, shows its evidence, lets her switch AI off and delete everything, and why our first ask is clinical sign-off, not more features.

Part 2 · How ReproSense works (what's in the backend)

One sentence: written rules decide; AI only handles language; everything is checked, logged and explained.

The path a record takes (the pattern pipeline, LangGraph)

Her entries (cycles, symptoms, pain, BP, labs, mood)→ 1 Intake: reads the record→ 2 Signal: computes features, runs the written rules (no AI)→ 3 Safety: red flags first; crisis stops here→ 4 Evidence: finds guideline passages→ 5 Writer: plain words (AI or template)→ 6 Verifier: drops unsupported sentences→ 7 Care gaps: opens or closes follow-ups

Every card shows this trace ("How this card was produced"): the rule, the numbers it used and each step with its timing.

Part 3 · The full question bank

A. The problem and the impact

ZHow big is the problem in the UAE?

Up to 70% of women with PCOS don't know they have it (WHO, 2026). In a UAE study of 518 women, endometriosis took 11.6 years to diagnose. In Al Ain, 21.3% of 2,546 pregnancies had gestational diabetes, and at one hospital only 265 of 1,043 mothers (25%) came back for the glucose test after birth.

ZWhy does the test after gestational diabetes matter?

Women who had diabetes in pregnancy have a much higher risk of type 2 diabetes later, and a third or more show raised blood sugar within a year of birth (34.5%, pooled from 18 studies, Endocrine 2025). The test finds it early, and it's what DoH measures clinics on in PC008.

YWhat outcome will you measure?

One number: the share of women with gestational diabetes who complete the glucose test within 12 weeks of birth, against the clinic's own baseline. Secondary: how long a clinician takes to understand a new patient's history.

YWhat's the cost saving?

We haven't measured one. /pilot shows the arithmetic with the clinic's own cost of a missed follow-up as an input, and it says it's not a forecast. The pilot gives the real figure.

ZWhy women's health and not general health?

Because the patterns are specific and slow: cycles, pain and bleeding only make sense over months, and they're the conversations women find hardest to have in ten minutes. It's also the hackathon theme we were assigned: women's, maternal and newborn health.

B. Clinical safety (DoH and clinicians)

KWhat happens in an emergency?

Red flags run before anything else and show "call 998 or go to the emergency department" first, with a one-tap call. Blood pressure of 160/110 or above in pregnancy or the 12 weeks after birth triggers it, following NICE NG133.

KWhat about false alarms, or missed ones?

We publish both. On held-out questions the everyday answers stayed at 42/50 with the AI check, so it added no new false alarms. Diagnosis and medicine refusals (6/10, 4/10) still miss sometimes, but those answers never contain a diagnosis or a dose; tightening them is next.

KWho is liable if something is missed?

It's an information tool for her and a summary for her doctor; the clinician makes every clinical decision. Our claims are frozen in writing, every rule cites its source, and red flags always point to 998. Liability terms go into the pilot agreement with the clinic.

ZWhy do the thresholds look like that, e.g. 21–35 days?

They come straight from the guidelines: the 2023 International PCOS Guideline defines irregular cycles that way for adults more than 3 years after their first period. Each card shows that sentence and the source.

ZDo you handle the gestational diabetes screen in pregnancy too?

Yes: at 24–28 weeks the app opens a follow-up for the glucose screen if it isn't done (ADA, NICE NG3), and after birth it tracks the test that DoH's PC008 counts.

ZHow do you handle postpartum depression?

The EPDS questionnaire, the standard one. A score of 10+ shows "worth discussing"; any answer above zero on the self-harm question goes straight to support with 998 and 800-SAKINA, never a score. The validated Arabic version still needs checking before real use.

KCould it scare women unnecessarily?

The wording is calm and specific: "worth discussing", with the exact days behind it, never a condition name or a percentage. Only true red flags use urgent language.

KDo you give medication advice?

No doses and no prescribing. Medicine questions are sent to her doctor or pharmacist with a source; on the held-out set that exact refusal happened 4 of 10 times and the other 6 gave general sourced information with no dose. That's a known gap we publish.

C. AI and technology (NYU Abu Dhabi)

KWhy rules and not a trained model?

Because a rule can be read, cited and signed off by a clinician, and DoH's Responsible AI Standard asks for traceability. We have no labelled real-world data to train on, and we won't publish a "73% risk" we can't justify.

A model could later rank which follow-ups need a nudge, but only after the pilot gives real, consented outcome data and only for workflow, not for diagnosis.

KHow does the search work, and in Arabic?

Hybrid search over our own summaries in English and Arabic: keyword search plus vector similarity, fused, then re-ranked so rare, specific terms count more. Arabic questions get Arabic answers; we tested 30 Gulf and standard Arabic safety probes and fixed 3 misses this week.

Postgres full-text plus pgvector with hashing embeddings (no external embedding service), merged and IDF re-ranked; stemming and Arabic stopwords; every chunk carries its source id.

KHow do you test the AI?

Three layers: a fixed 60-question set that runs on every change; a held-out set of 132 questions written separately and never tuned on; and a model safety score run as a separate job and published. Misses are listed, not hidden.

KWhat about prompt injection, e.g. instructions hidden in a lab report?

Uploaded text is treated as data, never as instructions, and the model's output passes the same guard. It's attack 7 in our suite, and it's blocked.

KWhat's the latency and cost of the AI?

Each request has a 10-second budget with one retry and a cache; most of the app needs no model at all. We haven't measured cost at scale yet; the pilot will.

KBias: does it work equally for everyone?

The rules are the same for every woman and come from international guidelines. The network view shows rates by nationality and age band with groups under 10 hidden, so a clinic can see gaps. A proper fairness review needs real data, so it's part of the pilot.

KWhat's agentic about it?

A 7-step pipeline (LangGraph) where each step has one job: read, apply rules, check safety, find evidence, write, verify, update follow-ups. A crisis stops the pipeline at step 3. Every step is traced on the card.

KCan it scale?

The API is stateless and the database does the access control, so it scales by adding servers. We fixed a connection-pool problem under load this week; we haven't load-tested at clinic-network scale yet.

KWhy Claude?

It was available to us and handles English and Arabic well, and it follows our strict output format. But nothing depends on it: the provider can be swapped, including for a UAE-hosted model, and the app works with AI off.

D. Data, privacy and regulation (DoH)

KWhich UAE rules apply?

The Health Data Law (Federal Law No. 2 of 2019: health data stays in the UAE), DoH's Responsible AI Standard, ADHICS for cybersecurity, and the Emirates Drug Establishment for device classification. Our /trust page maps each clause to what's built and what's planned.

KPeriod apps have sold data before. Why trust you?

No advertising or analytics trackers, and we never sell health data; it's in our pricing because clinics pay, not advertisers. She can see every access in her own audit log, export or delete everything, and use discreet mode.

KHow is consent handled?

Nothing is shared by default. She chooses to share with a clinic, can stop at any time, and the database enforces it: a clinician's query for a non-consenting woman returns nothing (attack 3, blocked).

KDoes it connect to Malaffi?

Not yet. The record already exports as FHIR R4, the standard exchanges use, validated in our tests; connecting through the proper DoH route comes after the pilot.

KWhat about DoH's new AI Clinician Framework?

That framework is for AI that performs clinical activities; we've designed ReproSense not to: it doesn't diagnose, treat or triage on its own. We'd still use DoH's living-lab route for the pilot.

KIs the data encrypted?

Yes: everything travels over HTTPS, and free-text notes and uploaded documents are also encrypted field by field (AES-256-GCM) inside the database. Names are removed before any AI call.

KHow do you protect her privacy? (20 seconds)

Hers by default: her clinic sees her record only if she shares, and she can stop sharing, export or delete it. The network sees counts, never names, with groups under 10 hidden. Names are removed before any text reaches a model; with AI off, nothing is sent. Every access is in a tamper-evident log she can read, and we never sell health data.

KWhat about a shared family phone?

Discreet mode shows "My notes" instead of the app name and hides her name on screen. She can give someone she trusts a read-only family link, choose whether it shows details, and revoke it at any time.

KWhat isn't done yet on privacy?

Said plainly: the demo runs on fictional data on free hosting in Frankfurt, with no backups, no penetration test and no privacy impact assessment yet. The ADHICS gap assessment is in December; UAE hosting and the penetration test are the February milestone, before any real record.

KWhat about minors?

The product is for adults 18+. Teenage periods have different guideline thresholds, and minors need a different consent model, so we don't cover them.

E. Business and adoption (PureHealth and investors)

YWhy is there no price?

Because it's a prototype and any number now would be a guess. Health buyers increasingly pay for measured outcomes, so we'll agree the price with our first pilot clinic once the pilot has measured the follow-ups closed. Women never pay.

YWhat's the market size?

Abu Dhabi has about 700 OB-GYN specialists, 14 fertility centres and 45 women's health facilities. We start with clinics, then networks and payers, then the GCC, and we'll size the revenue with pilot numbers, not a big top-down figure.

YWho's your first customer?

A primary-care or women's health clinic that sees women in pregnancy and after birth, for example one clinic in a large network such as SEHA. Primary care fits best because PC008 counts family-medicine visits.

YHow do you get women to use it?

Through their clinic, not ads: the clinic shows a QR code at the visit and she starts her own record. That keeps acquisition cost low and gives the clinic a reason to push it.

YWhat would an insurer like Daman get?

Follow-ups closed on a measured indicator, and counts by clinic without names. We'd start with a provider pilot and bring the numbers to a payer afterwards.

YWhat would you do with the grant?

Fund the pilot: UAE hosting, the penetration test, a clinical advisor's time and the first clinic's onboarding. The plan matches the grant in three stages over six months.

YIs it expensive to run?

No: most of the app needs no AI model at all, and a small UAE-hosted pilot server is a modest monthly cost. The margin depends on the price we set after the pilot.

KWhat's defensible if a big player copies you?

Cited clinical rules with sign-off, published safety evidence, the Arabic work and the tie to DoH indicators take time to rebuild. And a big player is more likely to be our channel than our competitor.

F. A founder's questions (Vitalls, health passport)

KWhy a separate women's app instead of a health passport?

A passport stores records. We add the clinical layer for women's health: cited rules, red flags first, the doctor report and the clinic follow-up. Her record exports as FHIR, so a passport could hold it.

ZWhy would she keep logging?

She doesn't have to log daily: two taps or one sentence by voice, and the payoff comes before her appointment, when a scattered year becomes one page. Reminders are opt-in.

YWhat traction do you have?

No customers or interviews yet, and we won't pretend otherwise. What we have: a live product built since acceptance, published safety results, a pilot proposal and a letter-of-intent template ready for the first clinic.

KWhat's the hardest thing you haven't solved?

Clinical validation with real women: we need a clinic, consent and an ethics route before any real data. That's why the pilot is the whole plan.

I. Curveballs

K"Your live cards say 'written from a template'. So where's the AI?"

The cards use the safe template by default; the AI does the language work where it helps her: voice into the form, lab photos, cited answers and the pre-visit brief. That's deliberate: rules decide.

K"Your data is in Frankfurt. Isn't that against UAE law?"

Only synthetic data is hosted there, for the demo. Real data will only be handled on UAE hosting, which is our February milestone, before any pilot.

Z"Isn't an 11.6-year delay an old figure?"

It's from a 2021 UAE study of 518 women; diagnostic delay is still measured in years internationally. We quote the local study because it's the closest evidence we have.

K"Why would a doctor read your report?"

It's two pages, built for a one-minute read: what changed since the last visit, the patterns with dates, and her questions. Whether doctors actually read it is something the pilot measures.

Y"Clinics are slow to buy software. Why now?"

DoH already measures clinics on PC008 and has published its Responsible AI Standard and AI Clinician Framework this year: the indicator gives clinics a reason, and the rules give them a safe path.
