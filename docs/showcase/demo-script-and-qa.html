Part C · Likely questions, with answers

1. How is this different from Flo or other period apps? Yash

"Flo and others are good at logging and predicting, and some now have symptom checks. We're built for the step after: turning her year into evidence her doctor can act on, with the exact days and the guideline behind each pattern, in Arabic and English, and following the care steps until they're done. And we never sell health data."

2. Why not just use Sahatna? Yash

"Sahatna is Abu Dhabi's front door to care: records, booking, teleconsults, and it already has maternity features. ReproSense is the women's-health specialist behind that door: it follows her cycles and pregnancy over months and explains each pattern with its guideline. We'd like to be that module, not a second app."

3. Do you diagnose? What if a pattern is wrong? Krishna

"No, never. A pattern means 'worth discussing with a doctor', and it shows the days behind it, so the doctor can check it in seconds. If she asks 'Do I have PCOS?', Nura says first that it can't tell, only a doctor can, and then gives cited facts to take to her doctor."

4. Where is the AI, and can it make things up? Krishna

"Rules decide the patterns, not the AI. The AI reads her voice and lab photos into fields she confirms, rewords text, and runs a second safety check that can only add an urgent route, never remove one. Nura's explanations are fixed sentences we wrote and checked against NHS and guideline pages, so they can't be invented, and every answer shows its source."

5. How do you know it's safe? Krishna

"Urgent signs are checked first, every time, and point to 998; a crisis message also gives DoH's 800-SAKINA line. We tested 132 messages, in English and four kinds of Arabic, written separately from the code and never used to tune it: all 30 crisis messages went to support and all 32 emergencies to 998."

6. What happens in an emergency? Krishna

"If she writes about heavy bleeding with dizziness, a severe headache in pregnancy or thoughts of harming herself, she sees 998 first, with a call button, before anything else. Red flags always come first."

7. Where is the data, and who can see it? Krishna

"It's hers by default. Her clinic sees it only if she shares, and she can stop sharing, export or delete. The network sees counts only, never names, and groups under ten are hidden. Names are removed before any text reaches a model. The demo is fictional and hosted in Frankfurt; production moves to UAE hosting, as UAE law requires."

8. How much will it cost? Yash

"There isn't a price yet, on purpose. Women always use it free. Clinics and primary-care networks subscribe, and insurers could later pay per follow-up completed. We'll set the number with our first pilot clinic, from what the pilot measures."

9. Have you tested it with real women or doctors? Yash

"Not yet. Everything you see runs on fictional data, by design. Our first 90 days are ten interviews, a clinic letter of intent and a consented pilot with up to 20 women at one clinic."

10. Is this a medical device? How will you get approval? Krishna

"It's built not to diagnose or treat, and our claims are frozen to information and organisation. We're preparing a device-classification memo for the Emirates Drug Establishment and an ADHICS gap assessment, before any real data."

11. What does the clinic gain? Is this a DoH measure? Yash

"The glucose test after diabetes in pregnancy is recommended 4 to 12 weeks after birth (American Diabetes Association), and many women miss it. Our clinic view follows each one from due to booked to done. DoH used to measure it as Jawda PC008; that indicator was retired in 2025, so our pilot clinic will confirm which current measure applies."

12. What's the impact? Zebo

"At one Al Ain hospital, on 2013 to 2014 data, only about 1 in 4 mothers with diabetes in pregnancy came back for that glucose test. We haven't measured our own effect yet. As a scenario only: if 100 eligible women went from 25% to 50%, that's 25 more completed follow-ups."

13. How good is the Arabic? Atharva

"Every screen and Nura's answers work in Arabic, right to left, and she can log in Gulf Arabic. A native medical reviewer still needs to check the wording; that's on our list before the pilot."

14. What is "What the Department of Health would see"? Atharva

"It shows, on our synthetic network, how many women's own entries match a pattern worth discussing, and how many have shared with a clinic, by region and age, counts only. It's for planning services, not a register or a count of diagnoses."

15. Does it connect to Malaffi or clinic systems? Krishna

"Not yet. Today she can export her record in a standard health format (FHIR). Connecting to Malaffi, with her consent, is on the roadmap with UAE PASS sign-in."

16. Isn't it too much in one app? Yash

"We start with one woman's problem: she can't explain what's been happening. The same record follows her as life changes, trying to conceive, pregnancy, after birth, but the pilot focuses on one clinic and one follow-up."

17. Longevity and fertility: how do you cover them? Zebo

"After diabetes in pregnancy, type 2 diabetes is about ten times more likely, so closing that follow-up protects her later health. For fertility, the app tells her when guidelines suggest asking for a fertility check: after 12 months of trying, or sooner from age 35 or with irregular periods."

18. What did each of you build? Krishna

"Krishna: the AI, backend and safety. Atharva: the web app and design. Yash: product and the venture side. Zebo: the women's-health evidence behind it."

19. What's next in six months? Yash

"December: interviews, a clinic letter of intent, the device enquiry. February: UAE hosting, UAE PASS and a penetration test. March to May: a consented pilot at one clinic and a licensed company."

20. Why should we believe the numbers on screen? Atharva

"Every person and number in the demo is fictional, generated by our synthetic data tool, and it says so on every screen. The real-world numbers on our slides each come with their published source."
